01 The call, decoded: why Pillar 1

UNICEF Femtech Ventures backs companies “using frontier technologies to build open-source solutions for the health, wellbeing, and sexual and reproductive health and rights of girls and women”: up to US$100,000 equity-free, 12 months of mentorship and access to UNICEF’s network, including governments.

The three pillars, and where Tara stands

PillarWhat the call asks forTara’s fit
1 · Healthcare access for women and girls
PRIMARY		 “The continuum of care, from antenatal and postnatal support…” · point-of-care tools with automated interpretation for frontline health workers · platforms linking screening, referral and follow-up · continuity of care “from conception through the first 1,000 days”, including remote care in low-bandwidth environments · tools covering community health worker workload. Exact match. A 24-question antenatal check-in, interpreted automatically by auditable rules, linked to the doctor’s referral and the mother’s follow-up, on a basic phone and a weak network.
2 · Informed, safe and accessible reproductive health
SUPPORTING		 “AI-powered accessible platforms… in underserved languages, especially for low-literacy and low-bandwidth environments” · CHW platforms for case management, referral and follow-up · consent- and privacy-preserving data. Sakhi is a voice AI in Kannada and Hindi: no typing, no English, no reading required.
3 · Mental wellbeing, safety and protection
SUPPORTING		 Decision support for CHWs on risk identification and specialist referral · tools for frontline workers. Risk flags + referral for the ASHA, and an SOS that reaches the nearest on-duty ASHAs.
Positioning line (use it everywhere: video, form, deck):
Tara is a Pillar 1 point-of-care platform: a voice AI that lets frontline health workers screen pregnant women in their own language, and links that screening to referral and follow-up, even on low bandwidth.

Eligibility, as the call states it

  • Working with a functional prototype — Tara runs today (ASHA, doctor and mother apps; voice check-in; SOS).
  • Committed to open-source licensing — add a LICENSE file to the repository (none exists yet) before you submit or show the repo.
  • Registered in a UNICEF programme country — confirm India on the programme-country list linked from the call page, and that the company is registered.
  • Safety-by-design is a condition of investment — the film states it; be ready to show consent screens and data handling in the application.
  • Women-led startups and founders under 35 are strongly encouraged — if that is you, say it in the form (and optionally in Act 4’s on-screen text).

02 The creative idea: a film that is its own proof

Most pitch videos describe speed. This one shows it. A small timer starts at 00:00 in the first frame and runs in the corner for the whole film. In that time the audience watches one danger sign, Lakshmi’s blood pressure, travel from a village register to a doctor and back to her phone. At 1:30 the timer freezes on the SOS screen. The film’s runtime is the argument.

One mother, not a market

UNICEF’s strongest demos are child- or facility-specific. Ours is mother-specific: Lakshmi D., 7 months, BP 148/96. Every screen in the demo is about her.

Three phones, one story

ASHA → doctor → mother. Each cut changes phone and colour of the UI, so a viewer with the sound off still follows the journey.

The ASHA is the hero

Sakhi helps; Sunita decides and acts. This answers “who uses it?” in the first 25 seconds and fits the call’s “designed within communities”.

Proof over promise

Every demo beat is a real screen from the working prototype, with real Kannada audio and real rule codes. No mock-ups in Act 3.

The emotional line, in four beats

BeatThe viewer feelsCarried by
Act 1Unease: the warning is real and written down, but it stays in a notebook.“That warning can wait days for a doctor.”
Act 2Relief: something simple, in her language, already in her hand.Logo resolve; three badges

Act 4Confidence: it is open, safe and ready to scale with UNICEF.“Every danger sign, heard in time.”

Brand line

Every danger sign, heard in time.

Used once, as the last spoken words, and on the end card. Do not use it earlier: the film earns it.

03 What UNICEF’s best demos do, and how Tara does it

The playbook’s analysis of shortlisted pitches (ClimaSalud, ClimaSense, EAGLE AI, SCP ChildShield) found four things every strong demo shows. The script hits all four, on screen, in Act 3.

Winning signalHow they showed itHow Tara shows it (time)
1 · Specificity (one child / one facility)A named school on a risk map One named mother, one reading: Lakshmi D., BP 148/96 (0:07 onward)
2 · Real-time automated alertsAlerts in under 30 s Rule PE-04 fires as she answers; the case is first in the doctor’s queue (0:58–1:20)
3 · Low-bandwidth / offline accessSMS, WhatsApp, USSD, basic phones Voice on a basic Android phone, low bandwidth, keeps working offline (0:35, 1:30)
4 · Open-source sustainabilityPublic repository, adaptable by governments “Tara is open source” with the repository on screen (1:30)
And what they avoided: tech-stack jargon. The script never says WebSocket, VAD, LiveKit or STT. It says “her words become text in about a second” and “one tap confirms the referral”: people doing things.

Closest benchmark: EAGLE AI (Kenya)

Voice-first AI in local languages, connected to real workflows. Tara goes one step further, and the film must make the difference visible: the voice does not end in a chat. It ends in a clinical rule, a doctor’s referral and a mother’s phone. That closed loop, screening → referral → follow-up, is literally a Pillar 1 bullet.

06 Read-through script (presenter copy)

The spoken words only, act by act, for rehearsal and the teleprompter. 264 words.

Act 1 · Problem 0:00 – 0:25

Watch the clock in the corner. Before this film ends, one danger sign will travel from a village to a doctor. Sunita is an ASHA worker. Her mothers live across three villages; their records live on paper. Lakshmi's blood pressure reads one forty-eight over ninety-six, with headache and blurred vision: severe pre-eclampsia. That warning can wait days for a doctor.

Act 2 · Solution 0:25 – 0:45

This is Tara: an open-source, voice-first maternal care network, built for the frontline health workers who already reach every mother. Its AI companion, Sakhi, talks with the ASHA in Kannada or Hindi, with no typing, and turns a spoken check-in into a screened clinical report the doctor can trust.

Act 3 · Live demo 0:45 – 1:30

Sakhi asks one question at a time, in a warm, familiar voice, and Sunita simply answers in her own words. Her spoken words become text in about a second. Transparent clinical rules, not a black box, flag severe pre-eclampsia on the spot, and every rule can be audited. When the check-in ends, Lakshmi is already first in the doctor's queue, with her vitals and the whole conversation. One tap confirms the referral. Clear, simple advice reaches Lakshmi in her own language. And if she is ever in danger at home, one SOS alerts the nearest ASHAs, her own ASHA first.

Act 4 · Scale & ask 1:30 – 2:00

Tara is fully open source. It runs on low bandwidth and keeps working offline, and it is safe by design: consent first, minimal data, and auditable rules. With UNICEF Femtech Ventures, we will pilot with two hundred ASHA workers in Bidar district, then open Sakhi to every Indian language. Every danger sign, heard in time.

Delivery notes

  • Say numbers the way you would to a friend: “one forty-eight over ninety-six”.
  • Pause one beat after “That warning can wait days for a doctor.” Let the register close in silence.
  • “ASHA”: say it as a word (AA-sha), never as letters.
  • Smile on “Every danger sign, heard in time.” and hold eye contact for two seconds after it.

09 Production, safeguarding and delivery specs

Safeguarding (a condition of UNICEF’s investment, so it is also in the film)

  • All data on screen is synthetic demo data (Lakshmi D., Sunita). Say so in the description: “All patient data shown is fictional.”
  • Anyone filmed signs a consent and release form, in Kannada, read aloud to them. No minors on camera.
  • No real patient registers, faces of patients, phone numbers or addresses anywhere in frame.
  • The SOS sequence is labelled as a prototype demonstration; it must not suggest it replaces 108 emergency services.
  • Do not use the UNICEF logo on the end card unless UNICEF has approved it; use the text line instead.
